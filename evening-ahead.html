The way you arrive matters, not as a performance, but as a gesture toward your own marriage and the other couples in the room.

The Language of the Room

Your masks are not costumes. They are a language, the most important one spoken in this room. Learn it before you arrive. Everything that follows depends on it.

Masks are one of the few things we ask of every couple on arrival, and the reason matters. When everyone arrives masked, no one is singled out. The couple choosing black, who simply wants to witness the evening, is not marked as different from anyone else in the room. The mask creates equality of presence from the moment you walk in.

This couple is present to witness, to feel the room, to exist inside the atmosphere together. They are not available for approach, conversation, or engagement of any kind. Masks can be changed at any moment, in either direction, with no explanation owed to anyone.

The Threshold

The Ring Ceremony

At a specific moment in the evening, the room gathers. This is not a performance. It is the moment the gathering crosses from social into something charged and intentional, the threshold between what the evening has been and what it is about to become.

Participation is not mandatory, but it is a beautiful way to see everyone in the room. Here is what happens so nothing surprises you:

The men form an inner circle. The women form an outer circle. You begin facing your spouse.

The inner circle, the men, rotate slowly, one position at a time. The women remain still. She is never moved, never circulated. She holds her ground and receives.

At each pause, a brief moment of eye contact is invited. Nothing more is required. If she opens her hands, he may gently place his into hers. If she does not, nothing happens. No explanation is needed. No gesture is wrong.

There is no talking. No performance. This is about presence, being seen and seeing, nothing more.

The ceremony ends when every man has returned to face his own wife. That final moment, his eyes finding hers again, is the point of everything that preceded it.

When the ceremony closes, the velvet rope is gone. The penthouse is the erotic field. All of it.

The loft. The terrace. The living room. The intimate spaces. Every room, every corner, every view of the city belongs to the Society from this moment forward. There are no wrong rooms after the ceremony closes. Wherever desire leads you, the space holds it.

If you want to shower, shower. If you want to draw a bath, draw a bath. If you want to take the night air on the terrace, it is yours. Nothing is expected. Everything is available.

Should you find yourselves drawn to one of the intimate spaces, a dark towel is already laid across the bed when you walk in. Aftercare towels are stacked on both nightstands, within reach from either side.

Take care of each other. That is what they are for.

The Open Field

Living Art

After the ceremony, you may find yourself in a space with other couples, or they may find themselves in a space with you. This is the nature of a shared erotic field. What it requires is one understanding above all others:

If you choose to be seen, you are, in that moment, living art. Beautiful. Sovereign. Completely yours, yet the room does not belong to you.

The spaces belong to Voluptas, and any couple choosing to express themselves are consenting to becoming beautiful live erotic art. They are there to be visually consumed. You don't need permission to witness.

What you don't do is interrupt. A couple in the middle of something is in their own world. You don't tap a shoulder, you don't hover waiting for eye contact, you don't ask. You either settle nearby and let your presence speak, or you move on.

If a couch or shared space is open in a room, it is yours. A bed is a different situation. The bed itself is sacred space. You cannot invite yourself onto it. If a couple wants company, they will make that known. The invitation comes from them, not from you.

Parallel expression near another couple, each couple entirely in their own world, proximity creating charge without contact, is one of the most natural things in this room. It requires nothing except reading the space correctly.

When a couple in a space turns toward you, you will know. Trust that. If an acknowledgment comes, follow it or don't. But do not manufacture it. Desire moves freely when no one feels cornered.

Your Responsibility to the Room

What You Bring

You are not an audience. You are part of the atmosphere. Every couple in this room contributes to the charge of the evening or draws from it. Come with curiosity, warmth, and presence.

  • Move at the pace of the slower partner. Always. The person who is less ready sets the speed, not as a constraint, but as an act of devotion. The most erotic thing you can do for each other tonight is refuse to rush.
  • If something feels heavy between you, take it to the vestibule or the terrace. Resolve it. Return when you are solid. The erotic field amplifies what is already between you. Come to it whole.
  • Nothing is expected. A completely valid and deeply beautiful Voluptas evening is one where you feel the room, feel each other, and return to your suite having touched no one outside your marriage. That is the product working exactly as it was designed.
  • Establish a private check-in signal with each other before you arrive. A word, a touch, a look that means come find me, I need a moment with you. Couples who have this move through the evening as a unit. Use it freely and without apology.
  • How to decline gracefully: a smile and returning your attention to your partner is a complete sentence. You owe no one an explanation for any boundary at any time.
  • Do not announce your intentions or your limits unprompted. Nobody needs to know what you plan to do or not do tonight. Let the evening unfold.
  • When you are ready to leave, you leave. There is no checkout, no farewell lap, no announcement. Collect your phone, step into the elevator, and carry the night home. The Irish goodbye was invented for a room exactly like this one.
